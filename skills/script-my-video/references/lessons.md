# Lessons from past scripts

General lessons that apply to anyone's script. Newest last. Format: date — rule — why. After each round of notes, add a line here if the lesson is general; if it's one creator's taste (their sign-off, humor level), put it in their voice profile instead. Write the why as a general reason, without per-video stats, titles, or view counts; those stay in the creator's own private tracker.

## Current guidance (reconciled 2026-10-31)

The dated entries below remain the learning history. Current instructions in `SKILL.md`, `shorts.md` and `youtube.md` qualify earlier blanket rules: a useful demonstration does not require an invented setback; title wording depends on the discovery surface; a one-word thumbnail or a feeling-only title is an option rather than a requirement; length and joke timing are starting choices. Keep explicit creator preferences separate from performance hypotheses. Review long-form with its own rubric, never Shorts 2-second/5-second predictions.


- 2026-10-10 — The first spoken words are the hook itself; no wind-up like "I actually tried…". — Most viewers decide in the first two seconds, and a hook that opens on the claim holds more of them than a wind-up, even against heavier graphics.
- 2026-10-10 — Lead with the human stakes; name the tool second. — Universal stakes need no context; a product or model name only lands with people who follow the news.
- 2026-10-11 — Write questions and talking points for the middle clips, not lines to read; only the hook and close are word for word. — Creators who riff sound natural answering a question and stiff reciting, and the edit only uses what they actually say.
- 2026-10-11 — Verify every number's origin before filming. — A striking stat nobody can trace back has to be re-measured or dropped, and finding that out after filming wastes the take.
- 2026-10-12 — Every clip opens with a word-for-word bridge line from the clip before, and the clips are filmed in order in one recording. — One-liners recorded separately across sessions cut together like random scenes: no line hands off to the next, and the edit can't invent the connection.
- 2026-10-12 — Plan one visual thread that runs the whole video and a transition out of every beat. — A new card on every beat resets the viewer every few seconds, even when the lines flow.
- 2026-10-13 — Write 1–2 punchlines per clip word for word. — Jokes depend on exact wording; a talking point like "make a joke about X" rarely lands when riffed.
- 2026-10-13 — Screen captures come before filming, and the script follows what they show. — A capture can contradict the plan (a status that reads "degraded", a timestamp later than claimed); finding that after filming means reshooting the line.
- 2026-10-18 — Run three idea tests before scripting: zero context, a setback and comeback, an open loop. — Most weak videos are weak ideas; no hook or edit fixes an idea a stranger can't follow or a story with no tension.
- 2026-10-18 — A hook opens a loop; it doesn't have to be a question. — Statements with an obvious "why?" hold viewers as well as questions do; what loses them is a hook that already gives away the result.
- 2026-10-18 — Write a separate title or caption per platform. — The same video can win in a swipe feed and flop in search, or the reverse; swipe feeds reward human stakes and YouTube rewards the words people search for.
- 2026-10-18 — When the creator has a specific receipt for the hook (a count, a dollar figure, a result), use it over a familiar frame like "you're doing X wrong". — Viewers have heard the generic frame many times and swipe before it lands; a specific number from the creator's own data is new to them.
- 2026-10-18 — Source ideas from the audience: a swipe file of the niche's top posts (borrow hook shapes, not content), unanswered questions in comments, and the viewer's own words for their problems. — Ideas pulled out of thin air guess at demand; the comments and top posts already show it.
- 2026-10-18 — Only make videos the creator has earned the right to explain. — Lived experience is what makes the video credible and uncopyable; a topic they'd need to research first reads like everyone else's.
- 2026-10-20 — For long-form, consider a true personal story as one option (not a default): the stakes are the creator's life and a fear the audience shares, the title names feelings plus a big true number, the thumbnail is one word over a real face, and the best twist is held until near the end. Don't bend tutorials or tool videos into it. — A sincere first-person story with universal stakes can travel on browse with almost no production, because viewers click to feel seen, not to learn a tool; forced emotion reads as fake.
- 2026-10-20 — A title for the home feed names a feeling the viewer already has, in the creator's true words; the search phrase moves to the description and chapter titles. — Identity lines ("I'm not a YouTuber") and how-to titles describe the creator or the task, so a browsing viewer has nothing to feel and no reason to click.
- 2026-10-20 — A long-form cold open is 20–30 seconds: the event or decision in the title's own words, how long the creator has been at it, the feeling or why it matters, and a one-line roadmap. Keep one-line punchy hooks for Shorts and mid-video re-hooks. — High-performing personal long-form videos open plainly on the event with a big true number of years; a Short-style zinger alone gives a browsing viewer no stakes for a 10-minute commitment.
- 2026-10-21 — Keep absolute words in opinions ("anyone can build anything") and exact words in facts (a duration, a number, what happened). — An arguable take fills the comments with debate, which helps the video; a checkably wrong fact fills them with corrections and costs trust, and hedging a take to "almost" just makes it forgettable.
- 2026-10-25 — Make the stakes the viewer's, not the creator's project. The creator's story is the receipt that proves a bigger fear the viewer already has (their job, their skills), and it should reach that fear in the first 60 seconds. — A long-form built around "my side project died" had no reason for a stranger to care; the creator called it "not impactful" until the spine became "is my job next?".
- 2026-10-25 — For "future of X" topics, script the creator's front-row view (what happened to them, the real headlines, what they're doing about it), not a forecast. — A forecast claims expertise the creator doesn't have and sounds like every other channel; first-hand receipts can't be copied.
- 2026-10-25 — Give every chapter of a long-form a named emotion (tension, nostalgia, joy, sadness, anger, hope) and check the creator's feelings actually appear on camera. Where the footage has none, plan pickups with the feeling left as a question for the creator. — Nine minutes with one understated feeling played flat even with strong visuals.
- 2026-10-25 — Family or kids the creator is hesitant to feature: one self-deprecating line at most ("my kids think I'm AI-pilled"), never a section and never on screen. — It adds warmth and stands in for the skeptical viewer without turning a career video into a parenting one or claiming expertise.
- 2026-10-26 — Every question the cold open asks ("Is software dead? Should you be scared?") gets answered on camera by name before the close, in its own line. — In the edit the creator had to ask whether the video ever answered its hook; the fix was stitching callbacks from scattered lines, which a planned answer clip avoids.
- 2026-10-26 — Plan the cover with the script: the question on the cover is the one the cold open asks and the video answers, and the shoot includes a few seconds of the matching expression (worried, skeptical) held to camera with the mouth closed. — A real frame with the right emotion beat AI-edited faces, and mid-sentence frames rarely have both the expression and a closed mouth.
- 2026-10-30 — When the creator competes in a test, they set the rubric, someone else writes the questions, independent people grade blind, and the result stays whichever way it goes. — A creator who writes the test already knows the answers, so viewers assume it's rigged, and an unseen question is the only way to film a real first reaction.
- 2026-10-30 — Hypothesis, not yet a rule: tie every stretch of long-form advice to a receipt, a test or a concrete question. — In one long-form, the weakest stretch was advice and opinion with no proof, and attention recovered when the advice became specific questions. A chapter card or viewer fatigue could also explain it, so check this against later videos.
- 2026-10-30 — Every video makes a claim and shows evidence for it, and opinion or advice is fine in any lane when it does. — A topic isn't weak because it's an opinion; it's weak when nothing backs it, so gather the evidence before filming, and for a big claim prefer a test it could lose.
- 2026-10-30 — Hypothesis, not yet a rule: give every philosophical point or piece of advice one specific thing that happened to the creator. — Across popular channels, opinion lists and skills advice held up when a real incident or painful personal moment carried them, while abstract versions of the same topics looked weaker.
- 2026-10-30 — When a story puts the creator on the side viewers resent, such as the boss doing the layoff, show what it cost them or say what others won't before asking for any sympathy. — Audiences of career videos side with the people affected, and a reveal works when the teller gave something up.
- 2026-10-30 — Score each idea against named rivals: could the two or three closest creators, or a generic channel, make this same video? — "Earned the right" can pass while a bigger creator still owns the topic, and naming them shows which of the creator's assets really can't be copied.
- 2026-10-30 — A lessons-learned video needs a hard cost and a "what I do now" viewers can check. — Without a cost it's a mild anecdote, and without a checkable change the ending turns into unproven advice.
- 2026-10-30 — Treat a creator's credentials as a line of proof and their public tools as receipts, not as the topic. — Viewers click for their own stakes, so a résumé or a tour of the creator's tools gives a stranger no reason to watch.
- 2026-10-30 — Read the creator's old posts before scoring or dropping an idea, and look for dated reversals. — A public claim followed months later by the opposite is a setback viewers can check, and it can already be on record when an idea looks unproven.
- 2026-10-30 — When ideas merge, the creator's own thesis stays on top and the other idea becomes its test or chapter. — The creator has to believe the video, and a test or chapter usually proves a thesis better than it stands alone.
- 2026-10-31 — Hypothesis, not yet a rule: in a long-form, stakes earn the click, what actually happened holds the viewer, and general advice loses them, even when advice is what the chapter promises. — On a much larger sample the weakest stretch stayed on reassurance and generic tips, and recovered when the advice became a specific question. It's still one video, and a chapter card sits at the same spot.
- 2026-10-31 — Before building a video on an opinion, check whether a past video already said it and what its retention did there. — A past curve is a free test of a message. A dip doesn't kill the idea, but it says the message needs a story and a cost, not reassurance.
- 2026-10-31 — Hypothesis, not yet a rule: on a home-feed title, test a specific event plus the viewer's stake against a title built on a feeling. This qualifies the 2026-10-20 feeling rule without replacing it. — In one test, the event version won on watch-time share. Title and cover changed together, so it can't say which one did it, but a feeling can belong to anyone, while an event gives the viewer something that happened and a reason it reaches them.
- 2026-10-31 — Before scripting a long-form, check it's a story and not an essay: the creator wants something the viewer can name, acts, gets blocked, and ends up changed, with a real scene in every chapter. Research and charts land as punchlines inside that story. — Most viewers come to be entertained, and a tidy argument can pass every evidence test while its protagonist disappears for minutes at a time.
- 2026-10-31 — Planning practice: complete the viewer want/payoff sentence in the Brief step before writing hooks, then locate the beat that earns it. — A concrete viewer desire keeps the promise, story and proof aligned; entertainment, recognition and perspective can be valid payoffs alongside practical instruction.
- 2026-10-31 — Keep the viewer want, title/thumbnail promise, proof and payoff aligned, and mark likely drop-off beats before filming. — A specific editorial risk is actionable; a fixed interruption timer or Shorts benchmark applied to long-form is not.
- 2026-11-03 — When a creator's own ventures carry a video, don't give each one its own chapter. Build one arc around a single turning-point scene, and bring the other ventures in as one flashback that proves one idea. — A chronological run through every project reads like a résumé or a laundry list. The creator rejected that shape, because the viewer follows a person who wants something and gets blocked, not a list of companies.
- 2026-11-03 — For a trend or "what's coming" video, show what already happened to the creator before predicting anything for the viewer, voice the skeptic's objection in the viewer's own words and grant what's true in it, then end on one cheap action the viewer can take this week. — Proof that already happened earns the prediction, a granted objection keeps skeptics watching, and a concrete next step turns worry into agency. Borrow the structure without the alarm: takes can be absolute, but capability claims stay exact. This is one guideline, not a template.
