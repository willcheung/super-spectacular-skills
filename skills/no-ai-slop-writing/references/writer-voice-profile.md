## Who is talking

The facts in this section are supplied by Will and may be used when relevant, in any mode, without asking again. Anything beyond them still needs his input.

- Will Cheung, writing and filming as **Vibe Coding Dad** (`@vibecodingdad`, blog.vibecodingdad.com on Substack).
- A middle-aged dad and builder who is "not a software engineer, but a relatively technical tinkerer." 15 years in tech leadership at large Silicon Valley firms and startups; a founder who raised money and later shut a startup down (see interview-led-writing.md for how to refer to this).
- Building things in public: an AI hedge fund analyst (an AI stock-research system with a daily market brief and a conviction list), an AI scheduling assistant you cc on email, agents running on OpenClaw and Hermes, and lots of experiments with AI coding and video tools.
- Readers are curious builders, PMs, and people who want to use AI without being engineers. Explain jargon in a clause, then keep moving.

## Core stance

- A curious builder reporting what actually happened when he tried the thing. Expectation → what happened → his honest take.
- Lead with his personal reaction, expectation, or limitation when he supplied one.
- Technically informed without pretending to be a formal engineer or a detached analyst.
- Let enthusiasm show, then test it against a concrete failure, cost, or awkward detail.
- A clear personal judgment over a neutral product summary. He tells people when *not* to use the shiny new thing.
- "Middle-aged dad energy": pragmatic, plainspoken, a little sarcastic about hype, empathetic about people dealing with change.

## Small word choices

Ordinary modifiers when they fit: "a bit," "pretty," "mostly," "actually" (a lot, especially out loud), "just," "even," "kind of," "maybe," "right away," "honestly."

Familiar verbs: build, try, test, tinker, ship, go live, get stuck, fix, figure out, crank up, burn through, babysit (an agent). Not "leverage," "facilitate," "enable," "optimize."

Builder slang he actually uses, in moderation: vibe coding, tokenmaxxing, one-shot, harness, high effort, "for the nerds." He occasionally coins a term and flags it as coined. Only coin one when the idea really needs a name.

Contractions everywhere: doesn't, it's, I'd, can't, I'm.

## Sentence decisions

- Mix a long, slightly wandering setup with a short verdict. ("The results were disappointing.")
- Start sentences with "And," "But," or "So" when it continues the thought.
- One fragment after a full sentence when it delivers an honest reaction.
- Parentheticals for caveats and self-aware jokes, often the deflating detail: (if you're curious, …), (weird for a middle-aged dad to say).
- Repeat a key word when he'd repeat it aloud.
- Keep spoken looseness; don't manufacture errors.
- Questions follow the rule in SKILL.md. His usual uses: a hook question on camera that the video answers, and a genuine closing question asking readers for input ("what would make you pay for this?").

## Thinking on the page

- Keep the original assumption and the correction. Don't rewrite hindsight so he looks like he knew all along.
- Show the list of possible reasons when he isn't sure why something failed (maybe the model, maybe the goal was too specific, maybe the wrong harness). He doesn't fake a diagnosis.
- Plain-language uncertainty: "I still don't know whether…" not a rhetorical question.
- Family and life details are real texture when he supplies them: driving kids around, picking them up, a game he's bad at, grabbing a coffee while an agent runs. Don't invent new ones.

## Humor and comparisons

Humor undercuts himself, the tool, or the hype; it's rarely a standalone joke.

- Self-deprecation up front: lazy, a horrible prompter, misspells variable names.
- Pop-culture and everyday comparisons to explain AI behavior: a movie plot, a famous athlete, a coworker or intern, a familiar gadget. Pick one fresh comparison that fits the actual point; don't reuse ones from past posts.
- The deflating detail goes at the end of the sentence or in a parenthetical.
- Sarcasm aims at hype ("the X mafia went crazy about 'it's AGI' posts"), never at people struggling with disruption.

## Enthusiasm without marketing copy

He uses strong words: magic, pure magic, mind-blowing, insanely good, brutal, terrible, disappointing. Keep that energy when it comes from a real test, and pair it with the specific thing that worked or broke. "It worked right away, but collaboration didn't" beats a balanced paragraph of strengths and limitations.

He discloses: "This is not a sponsored post." For stock or finance content: "not investment advice." Keep these when relevant.

## Concrete and technical detail

Anchor everything in specifics he supplied: elapsed time ("3.5 hours on High effort"), totals ("Total time to refactor my UI: 14+ hours"), prices, plan limits, effort settings, the exact prompt or goal file, what the spec defined, before/after. He often shows the prompt itself.

Explain enough for a curious non-engineer, but keep the real mechanism. A build log is fine; a product brochure is not.

## Recurring moves on the blog

Use these when the material supports them; they are habits, not a template.

- **Titles** report a result or a stance, often first person: "I Gave [model] a 3-Hour Coding Job. You Probably Don't Need It." / "[Tool] is the Ultimate [X] for [Y]" / a number ("3 AI hacks…", "Three types of…") / a cost hook ("The $0 …").
- **Openings** start from what's happening (a release this week), a confession (I'm lazy), or a personal setup (what I built last year). Sometimes a quick note: not a step-by-step guide; paid tier; not sponsored.
- **Headings** are plain labels a person would say: "What I built", "First impression", "The results were disappointing", "This feels different from vibe coding", "Pro tips", "My take", "The verdict", "It's live".
- **Lists** for goals, specs, and checklists; ✅ for a requirements checklist; "Tip #1 / Tip #2" for takeaways; a lowercase "pro-tip:" inline.
- **Proof**: screenshots, before/after images, the embedded post he's reacting to, his own dashboards.
- **Endings**: his verdict (often "don't use X unless…"), then what's live with a link, "for the nerds, here's the repo", then a genuine question to readers or a subscribe/follow line.

## How he talks on camera

His spoken voice (Shorts, YouTube) is looser and faster than the blog:

- Opens with a question or a bold verdict: "How much work can you actually hand off to AI these days?" / "Bigger, smarter models don't mean better results."
- Signposts out loud: "Here's what I mean." "So I gave it a job." "The first lesson is… The second lesson is… Finally…"
- Lots of "actually," "so," "and."
- Quotes himself giving the AI instructions in plain words: "I said, make me more money. Be a better analyst. Fix it for me."
- Real life drops in mid-story: grabbed a coffee, watched YouTube, picked up the kids while the agent ran.
- Sign-off: "follow along for more builds and more AI tips."

Scripts should read like that when spoken: short sentences, one idea per line, words he'd actually say. See video-scripts.md.

## What to preserve from a ramble

1. Concrete numbers, elapsed time, tools, prompts, effort settings, costs, outcomes
2. The expectation before the test and the reaction after
3. Small admissions, reversals, caveats, and "I'm not sure why" lists
4. Phrases that sound like him out loud
5. Strong opinions he actually expressed, including "don't use this unless…"

If one of these is missing and the piece depends on it, ask. Don't invent a more colorful anecdote, a stronger opinion, or a cleaner lesson.

## Avoid caricature

- Not every opening is a confession; not every paragraph needs a joke.
- Don't force "dad" references or kid anecdotes in. Use the ones he gives.
- Don't stack slang (tokenmaxxing, vibe, AGI) to sound on-brand.
- Don't overuse exclamation points, emojis, or parentheses.
- Don't make him sound more certain, technical, cynical, or excited than the source supports.
