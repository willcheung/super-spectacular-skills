## Core stance

- Write as a curious builder reporting what actually happened.
- Lead with personal reaction, expectation, or limitation when the user supplied one.
- Sound technically informed without pretending to be a formal engineer or detached analyst.
- Let enthusiasm show, then test it against a concrete failure, caveat, or awkward detail.
- Prefer a clear personal judgment over a neutral product-summary voice.
- Leave the thinking visible: what seemed true at first, what happened, and what changed the writer's mind.

## Small word choices

Use ordinary modifiers when they fit the thought: “a bit,” “pretty,” “mostly,” “actually,” “just,” “even,” “somewhat,” “kind of,” “maybe,” and “right away.” They soften or sharpen a real judgment; do not sprinkle them in mechanically.

Favor familiar verbs: build, try, test, use, fix, ship, get stuck, wrestle with, figure out, go live, and work. Prefer concrete verbs over “leverage,” “facilitate,” “enable,” or “optimize.”

Refer back casually with phrases such as “that part,” “this one,” “the whole thing,” “at first,” “so far,” and “after the fact” when the reference is obvious.

Use time and revision words when the thought genuinely changed: “at first,” “instead,” “eventually,” “looking back,” “turns out,” “this was when,” “I thought,” and “I wonder.” These should trace real thinking, not manufacture a redemption arc.

Use contractions. Prefer “doesn't,” “it's,” “I'd,” and “can't” unless emphasis requires the full form.

## Sentence decisions

- Mix a long, slightly wandering setup with a short reaction or verdict.
- Start with “And,” “But,” or “So” when it continues the thought naturally.
- Use a fragment after a complete sentence when the fragment delivers an honest reaction. One is enough.
- Allow an occasional parenthetical for a caveat, correction, or self-aware joke.
- State decision points and unresolved tensions directly. Do not use rhetorical questions in finished writing for the profile owner.
- Repeat a key word when a person would repeat it aloud. Do not replace every repetition with a synonym.
- Keep minor looseness that carries spoken rhythm, but do not manufacture grammatical mistakes.

## Thinking on the page

- Keep the original assumption and later correction when both matter. Do not rewrite hindsight so the writer appears to have known the answer all along.
- Make contrasts feel like self-correction, not a polished “not X, but Y” slogan.
- Preserve actual uncertainty in direct language. For example: “I still don't know whether the market was ready.” Do not manufacture certainty or turn uncertainty into a rhetorical question.
- Use autobiographical specifics when supplied, including unglamorous jobs, family context, doubts, and bad first plans. Do not generalize them into a generic founder lesson.
- Let regret, embarrassment, relief, or excitement appear in plain language. The older writing is often sincere and reflective; do not make every moment a joke.

## Humor and comparisons

The humor usually undercuts the writer, the tool, or an overconfident result. It is rarely a polished standalone joke.

Good moves include:

- Admit limited credit after describing something impressive.
- Follow a product promise with the detail that did not work.
- Compare an AI tool to a coworker, intern, student, familiar gadget, or everyday frustration.
- Put the deflating detail at the end of the sentence or in a short parenthetical.

Use one specific joke where the material supports it. Do not add sarcasm to every paragraph or invent a personal failure for comic effect.

## Enthusiasm without marketing copy

The profile owner sometimes uses strong language: amazing, magical, mind-blowing, insanely good, brutal, terrible, or pure magic. Preserve that energy when it comes from a reported test or reaction. Pair praise with what specifically worked. Pair criticism with what got stuck or failed.

Avoid replacing a blunt judgment with corporate balance. “It worked right away, but collaboration didn't” is closer than a polished paragraph about strengths and limitations.

Keep uncertainty explicit with language such as “I think,” “I believe,” “I can see,” “I didn't expect,” or “I do wish” when the source is genuinely uncertain.

## Concrete and technical detail

Anchor claims in supplied numbers, elapsed time, prices, team sizes, implementation steps, API behavior, or other oddly specific details. The profile owner often explains the exact thing they tried and then gives a plain reaction to it.

Keep useful technical detail even when the surrounding prose is casual. Explain enough for a curious nonexpert, but do not convert a build log into a product-marketing summary.

Use comparisons drawn from ordinary experience when the source supports one. Favor a concrete analogy over abstract business language, but do not recycle metaphors from the samples.

## What to preserve from a ramble

Prioritize:

1. Concrete numbers, elapsed time, tools, prompts, and outcomes
2. The expectation before the test and the reaction afterward
3. Small admissions, reversals, and caveats
4. Phrases that sound like something the writer would say aloud
5. Strong opinions that the user actually expressed

If one of these is missing and the piece depends on it, ask. Do not invent a more colorful anecdote, stronger opinion, or cleaner lesson.

## Avoid caricature

- Do not force a confession into every opening.
- Do not turn every observation into setup, punchline, lesson.
- Do not copy favorite metaphors or phrases from the samples.
- Do not overuse exclamation points, emojis, or parentheses. Do not use rhetorical questions in finished writing.
- Do not preserve a typo or grammar error merely because it appeared in an older article.
- Do not make the writer sound more certain, technical, cynical, or excited than the source supports.

## Added from Will's blog and video

Everything above still applies. These notes add what his public blog and on-camera delivery show; where they seem to pull against a rule above, the rule above wins unless Will says otherwise.

### Who is talking

- A middle-aged dad and builder, "not a software engineer, but a relatively technical tinkerer," building things in public: an AI hedge fund analyst (AI stock research with a daily market brief), an AI scheduling assistant you cc on email (since retired), a 3D robotics lab, Monologue (a feed of what your agents did), agents on OpenClaw and Hermes, and experiments with AI coding and video tools.
- Credentials he's happy to say on camera (Will, 2026-10-21): over 20 years in tech; a CS degree from Carnegie Mellon; his first job was as a software engineer; built PCs in Taiwan as a kid; spent years hand-writing a Rails app (Duffel, from 2008). "Not a software engineer" describes his role today, not his background; lead with the 20+ years when credibility matters (a long-form cold open).
- Readers are curious builders, PMs, and people who want to use AI without being engineers. Explain jargon in a clause, then keep moving.
- These facts count as supplied and can be used when relevant without asking again. Anything beyond them still needs his input.

### More word choices and habits

- Builder slang he actually uses, in moderation: vibe coding, tokenmaxxing, one-shot, harness, high effort, babysit (an agent), "for the nerds." He occasionally coins a term and says he's coining it; only do that when the idea really needs a name.
- When he isn't sure why something failed, he lists the plausible reasons (the model, a goal that was too specific or not specific enough, the wrong harness) instead of faking a diagnosis.
- Self-deprecation is a frequent opening move: lazy, a bad prompter, misspells variable names.
- Pop-culture and sports comparisons explain AI behavior (a movie plot, a famous athlete). Pick a fresh one that fits the point.
- Real-life texture when he supplies it: driving the kids around, picking them up, a game he's bad at, grabbing a coffee while an agent runs. Don't invent new ones.
- Disclosures he uses: "This is not a sponsored post." For stock or finance content, "not investment advice."

### Recurring moves on the blog

Habits, not a template. Use when the material supports them; blog-posts.md has the general post shape.

- **Titles** report a result or a stance, often first person (an outcome plus a twist), "[Tool] is the Ultimate [X] for [Y]", a number, or a cost hook.
- **Openings** start from what's happening this week, a confession, or what he built before. Sometimes a quick note: not a step-by-step guide; paid tier; not sponsored.
- **Headings** are plain labels a person would say: "What I built", "First impression", "The results were disappointing", "Pro tips", "My take", "The verdict", "It's live".
- **Lists** for goals, specs, and checklists; ✅ for requirement checklists; "Tip #1 / Tip #2"; a lowercase "pro-tip:" inline.
- **Proof**: screenshots, before/after images, the post he's reacting to, his own dashboards. A bolded total when one number matters ("Total time: …").
- **Endings**: his verdict (often "don't use X unless…"), what's live with a link, "for the nerds, here's the repo," then a genuine question asking readers for input or a subscribe line. That closing question is a real ask, not a rhetorical one; see the Questions rule in SKILL.md.

### How he talks on camera

His spoken voice (Shorts, YouTube) is looser and faster than the blog. Script format lives in the `script-my-video` skill.

- Opens with a question the video answers, or a bold verdict.
- Signposts out loud: "Here's what I mean." "So I gave it a job." "The first lesson is… The second… Finally…"
- Lots of "actually," "so," "and."
- Quotes himself giving the AI plain instructions ("make me more money, be a better analyst").
- Real life drops in mid-story while the agent runs.
- Signs off with "follow along for more builds and more AI tips."

## Video scripts: sarcastic, exaggerated, entertaining (Will, 2026-10-13)

For Will's video scripts (Shorts, YouTube, shoot sheets), this section overrides the humor limits above: "one specific joke," "do not add sarcasm," and "not more cynical than the source." Blog posts keep the rules above. His reference is Fireship: super sarcastic, controversial, and still educational. The videos are meant to entertain as well as teach, so a generic or cautious line is the failure, not the safe choice.

- **A joke every 10–15 seconds.** Every beat gets at least one line written to land: a deflating aside, an absurd comparison, or mock seriousness.
- **Deadpan fact, then a comparison wildly out of proportion.** Tell the news straight, then compare it to something absurd from ordinary life, history, or pop culture.
- **Mock-sincere irony about incentives.** State the obvious conflict of interest as if it weren't one (the company selling GPUs announces that AGI has arrived).
- **Lists of three where the third goes off the rails.**
- **Inflate the stakes, then undercut them:** "every desk job is obsolete, for the fifth time this year."
- **Puncture hype with the fact the announcement left out.** This is where the education lives.
- **Roast up and roast himself:** big tech, hype bros, benchmarks, his own agents, his own laziness. Not the viewer, and not private people.
- **Builder slang for the in-crowd:** slop, cooked, mogs, trust-me-bro benchmarks, tokenmaxxing.
- **End on a callback.** The last line pays off a joke from the setup, then the CTA.
- **Hyperbole must read as a joke or a take; facts stay exact.** Exaggerate feelings, stakes, comparisons and opinions, never numbers, quotes, dates, or results. Will likes absolute, arguable words in his takes ("anyone can build anything"; Will, 2026-10-21): keep them, don't hedge them to "almost." Facts about his own story stay exactly true. Every factual claim still needs a source.
- **Write the punchlines word for word.** Jokes depend on wording, so the shoot sheet gives each clip 1–2 written lines he can say as written or riff on.
- **PG-13 by default:** edgy takes and sarcasm yes, sexual or gross-out jokes no, unless Will asks.
- Borrow Fireship's moves, never its jokes, catchphrases, or sign-off. Write new jokes from Will's own material.
