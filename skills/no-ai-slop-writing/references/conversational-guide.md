## Sound spoken, not polished

Write like someone is talking through an idea with a smart friend. The result can be clear without being grammatically perfect.

Useful conversational traits include:

- Starting a sentence with “And,” “But,” or “So”
- Using “I think,” “like,” “probably,” “I mean,” or “honestly” when they carry real voice
- Joining closely related thoughts instead of polishing each one into a declaration
- Repeating a word when a person naturally would
- Using a fragment when the missing words are obvious
- Adding a quick aside, correction, or dry comment
- Choosing a familiar expression over technically pristine phrasing

Do not manufacture errors. Keep imperfect grammar when it improves rhythm, character, or honesty. Fix it when it makes the meaning hard to follow.

## Examples

Too composed:

> The tool generated a daily summary. However, I forgot most of it almost immediately.

More conversational:

> The tool made me a daily summary. Great. But I forgot most of it like an hour later.

Too polished:

> New technology often encourages me to reconsider ideas from the past.

More conversational:

> New technology keeps making me revisit old ideas, like the side project I abandoned years ago.

Too formal:

> The information frequently disappears into disconnected systems where nobody can retrieve it.

More conversational:

> The information usually goes into a black hole somewhere and nobody looks at it again.

Too tidy:

> I prefer immediate progress, although I recognize that preliminary questions improve the result.

More conversational:

> I want it to start immediately. But then it asks a few annoying questions, and I know it’s probably right.

## Preserve the person

Notice before editing:

- Does the writer hedge or speak bluntly?
- Do they use dry humor, self-deprecation, sarcasm, or none of those?
- Do they prefer short thoughts or wandering explanations?
- Which details feel like something only this person would know?
- How polished is their natural writing?

Preserve those signals. Do not add sarcasm, slang, profanity, or verbal tics that are missing from the source.

When no draft or voice sample exists, take tone cues only from the user's brief and the task context. Default to clear, conversational language. Do not invent a personal history or impersonate a specific person without source material.

When several voice samples exist, build a small working profile from recurring evidence: how the writer opens, how they explain technical details, how strongly they judge things, where they use humor, and how they handle failures or caveats. Separate stable voice traits from topic-specific formatting. Apply the profile during the current task; do not paste source passages or force every observed habit into the new piece.

## From ramble to draft

A ramble is source material, not a bad draft. Read it for four things:

- **Spine:** The thought the writer keeps returning to
- **Live wires:** Specific phrases, reactions, tensions, or examples carrying real energy
- **Support:** Facts, experiences, reasoning, and evidence already available
- **Open loops:** Places where an important connection, example, opinion, or conclusion is missing

Organize and expand the spine without flattening the live wires. Safe expansion includes spelling out an implication, connecting two supplied observations, explaining a term, or proposing possible directions as brainstorms. Unsafe expansion includes inventing why the writer cared, how something felt, what happened, what another person thought, or which conclusion the writer prefers.

When an open loop controls the piece, ask a concrete question tied to it. “What happened when you tried it?” is more useful than “Can you provide more context?” Keep the first round to the few questions that would change the draft most.

If the user asks to brainstorm, generate options freely but label them as options. Bring an option into the finished draft only after the user selects it or the supplied material already supports it.

## Avoid fake casual

Fake casual writing has its own formulas:

- “Okay, but hear me out” as a default opener
- “Plot twist” before an unsurprising fact
- A parenthetical joke in every paragraph
- Random lowercase text
- Forced slang
- An “I think” mechanically added to every claim
- Choppy fragments pretending to be personality

The goal is not to make the copy messy. The goal is to stop sanding away the human voice.

## Short-form writing

Give a short post one useful idea. It can be:

- Something that happened
- One test result
- An annoying failure and the fix
- A comparison grounded in the same task
- A genuine question
- A small observation with enough context for a stranger

Vary the shape. Do not end every post with a question or force every thought into a lesson.

## Long-form editing

Long articles often accumulate a different kind of slop: the same mechanism explained twice, every section built to the same length, and a conclusion that repeats the headline in grander language.

Edit those pieces surgically before rewriting whole passages. Preserve the writer's specific nouns, technical details, private shorthand, odd jokes, and sentences with natural roughness. Those details carry more voice than an added conversational phrase ever will.

Do not optimize prose for an AI detector. Detector scores are heuristic and can punish clean human writing or reward artificially noisy writing. Use a score as one signal alongside the actual editorial questions: Is the piece specific? Does it sound like this writer? Is it clear? Is it true?

## Final read

Ask:

1. Would the writer say this aloud?
2. Does it preserve the source's facts and uncertainty?
3. Can a stranger understand it?
4. Did the edit keep distinctive details and rough edges?
5. Is any humor revealing character, or merely performing it?
6. Can the last sentence be deleted?
